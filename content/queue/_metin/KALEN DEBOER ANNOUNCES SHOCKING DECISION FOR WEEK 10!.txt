First, let me be upfront about one thing. DeBoer hasn’t made a single dramatic announcement this week. There’s no bombshell press release. But in a week like this, the biggest decisions aren’t announced at a podium. They’re made in the meeting room, in the training room, and sometimes on the sideline ninety minutes before kickoff. And those decisions might shape this game more than anything else.

Let’s start with DeBoer’s scouting report.

On Wednesday night, DeBoer was asked about LSU, and he didn’t hold back. He called the Tigers an “all-around complete football team.” He said, “I just think there’s an attacking mindset that the best teams in the country have.” On defense, he said, “it’s complete across the board, so it’s not like you have a weakness.” And he added something really interesting: “I think the secondary gets overlooked because of what they do up front.”

That’s a big point. Everybody’s been talking about LSU’s pass rush, and for good reason. The Tigers have thirty-four sacks this season, the second most in the country. Princewill Umanmielen has nine sacks, and he and Jordan Ross have combined for thirteen and a half. But DeBoer is telling you the back end is just as good. And the numbers back him up. LSU’s pass defense ranks third in the SEC, and it has reportedly allowed just four passing touchdowns all season while intercepting seven passes. That’s an elite unit.

And DeBoer also said this: “They got creative schemes. They do a good job of disguising and making it tough on your quarterback.” So this isn’t a defense that just lines up and rushes four. This is a defense that’s going to try to confuse Keelon Russell, show him one look before the snap and give him something completely different after it.

Then DeBoer turned to LSU’s quarterback, Sam Leavitt, and this was my favorite part. He said, “There’s a lot of plays that are dead in the backfield,” and then Leavitt “somehow ends up going right and ends up in the end zone.” Think about that. Alabama’s head coach is basically saying that the most dangerous moments with Leavitt come after the play breaks down.

And that matches what an anonymous defensive coordinator told Saturday Down South back in August. He said Leavitt is “sneaky athletic, really way more elusive than you would think.” And he added, “where he gets you is when he extends plays.” That’s exactly what DeBoer sees on film. Leavitt has thrown seven touchdown passes with no interceptions in his last two games, and he has eight rushing touchdowns this season, five of them at Tiger Stadium. This is a quarterback who can beat you with his arm and his legs, especially at home.

So that’s the scouting report. A complete team. An attacking mindset. A disguising defense with an underrated secondary. And a quarterback who makes plays out of nothing. Now, here are the decisions DeBoer has to make to beat it. But before we get there, let’s remember that Alabama has a few things LSU has to worry about too. Russell has thrown at least fifteen touchdown passes through eight games. Alabama’s tight end room, Pritchett, Edwards and Ford, has become one of the most reliable red-zone groups in the country. Rico Scott is coming off a career game. And Alabama’s defense held Texas A&M to fifteen total yards in the fourth quarter two weeks ago. Kiffin is sitting in his office watching that film too, and he knows Alabama can hurt him in a lot of different ways. So this isn’t a one-sided scouting report. Both coaches have a lot to prepare for.

Decision number one. Ryan Coleman-Williams.

This is the biggest one. Coleman-Williams, Alabama’s top receiver, had arthroscopic surgery on a meniscus injury after the Georgia game on October tenth. He’s missed the last two games. He’s been listed as questionable on both the Wednesday and Thursday availability reports. DeBoer said back on October twenty-sixth, “I’m hopeful that he’ll be ready for the LSU game.” And even Lane Kiffin said this week, according to Tiger Rag, “I bet Ryan will be back.”

So the question isn’t just, does he play? The question is, how much does he play, and how do you use him?

Here’s what I think the staff is weighing. On one hand, Coleman-Williams is the kind of receiver who changes how a defense plays. Before the injury, he had twenty-three catches for four hundred fifteen yards and four touchdowns, eighteen yards per catch. Against a defense that DeBoer says disguises everything, having a receiver who can win one-on-one deep forces LSU to keep safeties back. That opens the middle of the field for Marshall Pritchett, Kaleb Edwards and Josh Ford. It gives Rico Scott, who just had six catches for one hundred fifty-six yards against A&M, more room to work. And it gives Russell a go-to target on third down.

On the other hand, there’s the long game. After LSU, Alabama has Vanderbilt on the road, Chattanooga at home, and Auburn in the Iron Bowl. Then, hopefully, the playoff. The staff is not going to risk Coleman-Williams’ long-term health for one game, no matter how big. So I could see a plan where he plays, but on a controlled number of snaps, maybe in specific packages. Use him in the red zone. Use him on third down. Take a couple of deep shots to make LSU respect him. And don’t overload him in his first game back. And if he can’t go, or if he’s limited, the staff has already shown it can win without him. Rico Scott stepped up as the top target against A&M. Lotzeir Brooks has been steady in the slot. Derek Meadows, Noah Rogers and Cederian Morgan give the room depth. And the tight ends have been reliable all season. So the decision on Coleman-Williams isn’t about whether Alabama can function without him. It’s about how much better Alabama can be with him.

And remember, this is a guy who changed his name this year to honor his mother and switched to number one, saying he wanted “an opportunity to have a hard reset.” He said, “I have a legacy that I want to write.” If he steps onto the field in Death Valley and makes a big catch, that’s a moment Alabama fans will remember for a long time.

Decision number two. Who’s at center?

Racin Delgatty has missed the last two games with a knee injury, and Mal Waldrep has filled in. Delgatty is listed as questionable. This decision might not get as many headlines as Coleman-Williams, but it might be even more important.

Here’s why. Through the first six games, with Delgatty at center, Alabama averaged around one hundred eighty-two rushing yards per game. In the two games without him, the Tide averaged about sixty-one. Now, there are a lot of reasons for that drop, but the center is the brain of the offensive line. Against an LSU defense that DeBoer says has “creative schemes” and does “a good job of disguising,” the center’s job is even harder. He has to identify the fronts, call the protections, and adjust on the fly while one hundred thousand fans are screaming. In a stadium that loud, the offense will likely be using a silent count, where the center and the quarterback communicate with hand signals and timing instead of voice calls. That’s a lot harder when the center is new to the role or not fully healthy. Every false start, every missed protection call, every busted assignment can turn into a sack against a pass rush this good. That’s why the decision at center matters so much.

So if Delgatty is healthy enough, I think he starts. His experience and communication are exactly what this line needs against this defense. But if he’s not quite at full speed, the staff has a solid option in Waldrep, who has battled through two tough games. The key decision is whether a partly healthy Delgatty is better than a fully healthy Waldrep. That’s a tough call, and it’s one the staff will make after seeing how Delgatty looks in warmups.

And then there’s William Sanders. The starting left guard had a TightRope procedure on his ankle after the A&M game. Back on October twenty-sixth, DeBoer said he’d be out “probably two to three weeks.” LSU is exactly two weeks after the A&M game. So Sanders being listed as questionable is a good sign, but I’d still call him a long shot. If he can’t go, the staff has to choose the best five healthy linemen and make sure they’re on the same page. Left tackle Jackson Lloyd summed up the group’s mindset this week: “Backs against the wall, everyone knows what we got to do.”

Decision number three. The secondary.

Zabien Brown, the preseason All-American cornerback, hurt his lower leg against Tennessee and sat out the A&M game. He’s questionable for LSU. And this decision connects directly to DeBoer’s scouting report, because LSU’s passing game is dangerous. Leavitt has weapons like Jackson Harris, who had one hundred sixty-three yards and two touchdowns against Texas A&M earlier this season, plus Trey’Dez Green and Winston Watkins Jr.

If Brown plays, Kane Wommack can match him up on LSU’s best receiver and be more aggressive with his calls. If Brown is limited or out, Wommack has to lean on the guys who stepped up against A&M. Djion Lee Jr., who had the pick-six. Chuck McDonald III, who had his first career interception. Carmelo O’Neal, who Wommack has said is getting better every single week. And safeties Bray Hubbard and Keon Sabb, who have to get everybody lined up.

And Hubbard already told us the plan. He said, “We got to do a great job disguising and making sure they can’t just tip our coverages off of just our alignment.” That’s the chess match. LSU disguises on defense, and Alabama has to disguise right back. Kiffin’s offense tries to read the defense before the snap. Wommack’s job is to make sure what Kiffin sees isn’t what he gets. And there’s another layer to this decision. If Brown plays but isn’t at full speed, does Wommack trust him to play press man coverage on LSU’s best receiver, or does he play more zone to protect him? That’s a game-time judgment call. A cornerback coming back from a lower leg injury might be fine running straight lines but could struggle changing direction against a quick route runner. The staff will be watching him closely in warmups to make that call.

Decision number four. The defensive line rotation.

Terrance Green, Alabama’s starting nose tackle, limped off late in the first half against Georgia and then reaggravated the injury. He’s missed the last two games. He’s listed as questionable with an ankle injury. But Yhonzae Pierre said on Tuesday, “We got TG coming back, Terrence Green, so we’ve been working on schemes for the defense and for them.”

If Green plays, the decision becomes how to manage his snaps. A nose tackle coming back from an ankle injury probably isn’t going to play sixty snaps. But even twenty or thirty quality snaps in the middle would be huge. It lets Fatutoa Henry, who had two sacks against A&M, and Devan Thompkins rotate and stay fresh. It keeps the interior strong against the run. And it frees up Pierre to do what he did against LSU last year, when he had two sacks and the strip-sack that sealed the game. And DeBoer also said Justin Hill is back, which gives the defensive line another body. In a game like this, with Leavitt extending plays, the pass rush has to stay fresh into the fourth quarter.

Decision number five. The backfield.

EJ Crowell, the true freshman running back, has been questionable with a lower leg injury. So has AK Dear, with an ankle injury. Crowell has four rushing touchdowns this season and is averaging six point eight yards per carry. Ryan Grubb has said he wants Crowell touching the ball at least ten times a game.

If Crowell is available, the decision is how much to lean on him. Against a defense with thirty-four sacks, a strong run game is the best way to slow down the pass rush. Run the ball, keep LSU’s defensive ends honest, and set up play-action. And with LSU’s star linebacker Whit Weeks expected to be out, according to CBS Sports’ Matt Zenitz, Alabama might have a chance to test the middle of LSU’s defense. Trae’shawn Brown and Daniel Hill have carried the load in recent weeks and done their part. But adding Crowell’s explosiveness would give Grubb another weapon.

Decision number six. The quarterback game plan.

And this one is all about Keelon Russell. DeBoer told us LSU’s defense disguises its looks and makes life tough on the quarterback. So what’s the plan? According to reports from Tuscaloosa, DeBoer’s message to Russell this week has been about reading down and distance, staying steady, and getting the ball out quickly. And DeBoer said, “I think it’s just learning from what you’ve been through.” He added, “That will come to him because he’s that type of player.”

So I expect a game plan built around quick decisions. Short and intermediate throws. Screens. Tight ends in the flat. Run-pass options that let Russell get the ball out before the pressure arrives. And then, once LSU starts sitting on those, one or two shots down the field, maybe to Coleman-Williams if he’s out there. That’s how you beat a defense that disguises. You don’t try to out-think it every snap. You take what it gives you and make it pay when it guesses wrong. And I wouldn’t be surprised to see Alabama come out with a fast tempo early. When an offense moves quickly, the defense has less time to disguise. LSU can’t rotate its safeties and swap its looks as easily if Alabama is snapping the ball quickly. That’s another way to take some of the guesswork out of it for Russell, especially on the opening drive, when the crowd is at its loudest.

And Russell has shown he can handle it. At Kentucky, he turned it over on his first two possessions and Alabama still won by twenty-eight. Against Florida State, he led a comeback from fifteen down. And against Texas A&M, he went five for five for sixty-eight yards and two touchdowns in the fourth quarter. When things get chaotic, this young quarterback has a way of settling down and making plays.

And on defense, the game plan against Leavitt is just as important. Kane Wommack said this week, “You’ve got to create enough doubt in their minds and then capitalize.” He added, “So far, we’ve found ways to capitalize and create takeaways off RPOs.” And remember what DeBoer said about Leavitt turning dead plays into touchdowns. That means Alabama’s pass rushers can’t just run past him. They have to stay in their lanes, collapse the pocket, and keep him from escaping to his right. If Alabama keeps Leavitt in the pocket and makes him throw into tight coverage, the turnovers will come.

Decision number seven. Game management.

This one doesn’t show up on an injury report, but it’s just as important. In a game this close, the head coach will face moments that decide everything. Do you go for it on fourth and two near midfield, or punt and trust your defense? Do you kick a field goal or go for the touchdown in the red zone? Do you use your timeouts before halftime to try to steal a score? And how do you handle the clock at the end of the game? These are the moments where a head coach earns his money.

And there’s an added wrinkle. Conor Talty missed two of his three field goals against A&M after making fifteen in a row before that game. Every kicker has an off day, and I fully expect him to bounce back. But if DeBoer is feeling a little less sure about a long field goal, that could change some fourth-down decisions. I think the smart play is to trust Talty. He’s earned it. But it’s something to watch.

So let’s add it all up. Seven decisions. Coleman-Williams’ role. The center. The secondary. The defensive line rotation. The backfield. The quarterback game plan. And game management. If DeBoer and his staff get most of those right, Alabama has a great chance to win in Baton Rouge.

Now, since I’m asking you what you would decide, let me tell you what I would do. On Coleman-Williams, I’d play him, but in a controlled role. Maybe thirty or thirty-five snaps, with a focus on third downs and the red zone, plus a couple of deep shots early to make LSU respect him. At center, if Delgatty can move well in warmups, I start him. His communication against those disguises is worth it. If not, Waldrep has earned the trust. At corner, if Brown is healthy enough to run and change direction, he plays, but I’d mix in more zone early until he proves he’s comfortable. On the defensive line, Green plays in a rotation, fresh every snap. In the backfield, if Crowell is available, he gets his ten touches. On offense, quick game, tempo early, and protect Russell at all costs. And on game management, I trust Talty, and I’m aggressive on fourth and short in LSU territory. That’s my plan. Of course, DeBoer and his staff see these players every day, and they know far more than any of us. Now let’s see what DeBoer does.

Now let’s talk about what’s at stake, because it raises the pressure on every one of those decisions.

The College Football Playoff committee ranked Alabama fifth this week, the highest-ranked two-loss team in the country. Not everyone agreed. On national TV, analysts asked how Alabama could be eight spots ahead of a Tennessee team that beat the Tide. Others argued for Texas and its wins over Ole Miss, Ohio State and Tennessee. And Paul Finebaum said, “If Alabama loses this game, forget it.” Those are fair debates, and Tennessee deserves credit for its win. But here’s the thing. The committee looked at Alabama’s road win at Kentucky, now ranked sixth, its blowout of South Carolina, now ranked twenty-first, and a three-point loss to number two Georgia, and decided this team belongs near the top. The best way to end the debate is to win on Saturday.

And for LSU, the stakes are just as high. One analyst described the teams ranked eleven through fourteen, including LSU, as being on the chopping block. The Tigers still have Texas at home and a trip to Tennessee after this. So LSU is going to come out swinging. This is basically an elimination game for both teams.

And here’s a bit of history to keep in mind. Under DeBoer, Alabama is two and oh against LSU, with a combined score of sixty-two to twenty-two. That includes a forty-two-to-thirteen win in Baton Rouge in 2024 and a twenty-to-nine win in Tuscaloosa last season. And LSU is just one and six in its last seven night home games against Alabama. This game is in the afternoon, which Kiffin wasn’t happy about. DeBoer’s response was perfect: “We’re on the road, so, you know, I’d rather play at home if we want to have all these advantages.” He added, “It is what it is. We just got to show up and play.”

That’s the mindset. No excuses. No distractions. Just go play. And honestly, I think that calm approach is one of DeBoer’s biggest strengths. He doesn’t get dragged into the noise. He doesn’t trade jabs in the media. He just prepares his team and lets the results speak. In a week where there’s been so much talk about rankings, kickoff times and injuries, that steady leadership is exactly what a young team needs heading into a hostile environment.

And one more thing on LSU’s injuries. LSU linebacker Whit Weeks, the team captain, broke his fibula back in September and is expected to miss this game. We never celebrate an injury, and we wish him a full recovery. But it’s one more thing Kiffin has to adjust to. And after Weeks went down earlier this season, Kiffin said, “I’ve not been around this many significant injuries early in the year like this.” Both teams are dealing with injuries. Both coaching staffs have tough decisions to make. And the team that manages its roster best might be the team that wins. Kiffin has his own decisions to make, too. With Weeks expected to be out, LSU has used Dahvon Keys at the second linebacker spot. Linebacker Zach Weeks, Whit’s younger brother, is questionable. So Kiffin and his defensive coordinator, Blake Baker, have to decide how much to trust their younger linebackers against Alabama’s play-action and tight ends. That’s a matchup I think Grubb will try to exploit.

So here’s my final breakdown.

On offense, Alabama needs to protect Russell against that pass rush, use quick throws to beat the disguises, run the ball well enough to stay balanced, and hit a big play or two when LSU guesses wrong. If Delgatty and Coleman-Williams are out there, that becomes a lot easier.

On defense, Alabama needs to contain Leavitt, keep him from extending plays, disguise its coverages, and take the ball away. If Green and Brown are out there, that becomes a lot easier too.

And on special teams, Conor Talty needs to bounce back after missing two of his three kicks against A&M. He made fifteen straight before that game, so I fully expect him to be ready. In a close game, every point counts.

My prediction hasn’t changed all week. Alabama twenty-seven, LSU twenty-four. I think DeBoer makes the right calls with his injured players. I think Russell executes the quick game well enough to keep the pass rush off balance. And I think Alabama’s defense makes one more big play than LSU’s does, just like it did against A&M. And when it’s over, Alabama heads home seven and two, with another ranked road win and a firm hold on its playoff spot.
